    THE SCIENCE OF MITOSIS

    REFERENCE SHEET · CELL BIOLOGY

    What mitosis is

    Mitosis is the division of one cell nucleus into two genetically identical nuclei. Its job is faithful copying: each daughter cell must end up with the same number and kind of chromosomes as the parent. In humans that means 46 chromosomes in, 46 chromosomes out.

    Mitosis is only the nuclear part. Splitting the surrounding cytoplasm is a separate process called cytokinesis, which is why this game treats it as its own stage.

    The five stages in this game

    1. Prophase — Loose chromatin coils into compact, visible chromosomes. The nuclear envelope breaks down and the two centrosomes travel to opposite poles, growing the spindle between them.
    2. Metaphase — Spindle fibres pull every chromosome into a single line across the cell's equator, the metaphase plate. The cell will not proceed until each one is correctly attached — a genuine biological checkpoint.
    3. Anaphase — Each centromere lets go and the two sister chromatids are hauled to opposite poles. From this moment each pole holds a complete chromosome set.
    4. Telophase — The chromatids relax back into chromatin, a fresh nuclear envelope assembles around each cluster, and the nucleolus reappears inside each new nucleus.
    5. Cytokinesis — The cytoplasm is finally split, giving two separate daughter cells.

    Animal versus plant division

    Animal cells have only a flexible membrane, so a ring of protein filaments tightens like a drawstring and pinches the cell in two. That inward groove is the cleavage furrow.

    Plant cells are boxed in by a rigid cell wall and cannot be pinched. Instead, vesicles line up along the equator and fuse into a cell plate that grows outward until it meets the existing wall, building a brand-new wall between the daughters.

    Where it happens

    Constantly, and everywhere that tissue grows or is replaced: the basal layer of your skin, bone marrow making blood cells, the lining of your gut, and the meristem at the tip of a plant root. When the controls on mitosis fail and division runs unchecked, the result is a tumour — which is why the metaphase checkpoint matters so much.

    Glossary

    Chromatin
    DNA wound around proteins in its relaxed, thread-like working state.
    Chromosome
    Chromatin condensed into a compact, transportable package.
    Sister chromatids
    The two identical halves of a duplicated chromosome, joined at the centromere.
    Centromere
    The pinched waist where chromatids are held together and where spindle fibres attach.
    Centrosome
    The organising centre that builds the spindle; one ends up at each pole.
    Spindle
    The framework of microtubules that moves chromosomes around the cell.
    Metaphase plate
    The imaginary plane across the equator where chromosomes line up.
    Nuclear envelope
    The double membrane enclosing a nucleus; it dissolves and later reforms.
    Nucleolus
    The dense region inside a nucleus where ribosomes are assembled.
    Cleavage furrow
    The inward groove that splits a dividing animal cell.
    Cell plate
    The new wall built across the middle of a dividing plant cell.
