Learn More — The Story of the Elements

The Chaos Era

By the early 1800s dozens of elements were known, but with no organising principle. Chemists could not predict how any element would behave, and there was no way to see the hidden relationships between them.

Dobereiner's Triads (1829)

Johann Dobereiner noticed groups of three elements with similar properties, where the atomic mass of the middle element was roughly the average of the other two — for example Lithium, Sodium, Potassium; Calcium, Strontium, Barium; and Chlorine, Bromine, Iodine.

Newlands' Law of Octaves (1864)

John Newlands arranged elements by increasing atomic mass and found that every eighth element shared similar properties, like the octaves of a musical scale. It worked for lighter elements but broke down for heavier ones, and was mocked at the time.

Mendeleev's Vision (1869)

Dmitri Mendeleev arranged elements by atomic mass into a table of rows and columns — and boldly left gaps for elements not yet discovered, predicting their properties. When Gallium, Scandium and Germanium were later found matching his predictions, the table's power was proven.

The Modern Table (Moseley, 1913)

Henry Moseley showed that the true ordering principle is atomic number (the number of protons), not atomic mass. This fixed the handful of pairs that sat awkwardly when ordered by mass — such as Argon and Potassium — giving us the modern Periodic Table.

How to Read a Plate

Each element plate shows its atomic number (top), chemical symbol (centre) and name (bottom). The colour of its inner glow marks its family: alkali metals, alkaline earths, halogens, noble gases, transition metals and more.
